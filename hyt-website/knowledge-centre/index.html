Guide 1

Understanding wheel wear

A railway wheel is not a simple cylinder. Its tread is coned or curved so that the wheelset steers itself through curves and centres itself on straight track, and its flange keeps the wheel on the rail when steering alone is not enough. Every kilometre changes that geometry a little. Re-profiling restores it before the changes affect safety, ride quality or track wear.

Most wear falls into a handful of recognisable patterns. Knowing which one you are dealing with – and why it occurs – helps decide how often to measure, when to intervene and whether the root cause lies in the vehicle, the brakes or the track.

  • Wheel flats – flat spots ground into the tread when a wheel slides under braking, typically on low adhesion. They cause impact loads that damage track, bearings and the vehicle, and noise that passengers notice at once.
  • Hollow tread – the running surface wears into a concave shape. The wheel then behaves as if it had a much steeper cone, which can cause unstable running at speed, higher contact stresses and a 'false flange' on the outer edge that strikes switches and crossings.
  • Thin flange – sustained contact with the rail gauge face, mostly in curves, wears the flange thinner. Below the operator's limit, the risk of the flange climbing or splitting points rises.
  • Sharp or vertical flange – the flange face wears steep and its root sharpens, which makes flange climbing more likely and damages switch blades.
  • High flange – as the tread wears down, the flange becomes relatively taller and can strike fishplates, bolts and crossing components.
  • Shelling, spalling and thermal cracks – surface fatigue and heat from tread braking break out small pieces of metal or leave fine cracks that grow if they are not machined away.
  • Out-of-roundness and diameter differences – polygonal wear and mismatched diameters on one axle or within one bogie cause vibration and poor steering.

Underfloor vs portal vs surface lathe: how to choose

The right machine depends less on the lathe itself than on where the wheelset is when it needs machining. If wheelsets stay in the vehicle, an underfloor lathe saves the most time. If they are already out – in a wheel shop or during overhaul – a portal or surface lathe is the natural choice.

Many operators combine both approaches: an underfloor lathe keeps the fleet running between overhauls, while a portal or surface lathe handles wheelsets removed during heavy maintenance.

More on selection

Single or tandem, one lathe or two

Once you know which family fits, the next question is capacity. A single underfloor lathe machines one wheelset at a time. A tandem underfloor lathe pairs two synchronised machines so that both wheelsets of a bogie are machined in one setting – the right choice when long trains must return to service within a tight window.

For wheelsets that are already removed, compare the portal wheel lathe, the roller-type portal wheel lathe and the surface wheel lathe. Base the decision on the number of wheelsets per shift, the range of diameters and axle lengths, whether brake discs must be machined and how wheelsets will reach the machine.

Size the installation for the fleet you will have, not only the fleet you have today. Adding a second machine later is far easier if the pit, track and services were planned for it from the start.

Guide 2

Planning an underfloor lathe pit

An underfloor wheel lathe becomes part of the building. The pit, the track above it and the services around it are fixed for decades, so the decisions made at design stage have a lasting effect on productivity. Bring the machine supplier in before the depot layout is frozen.

Use the checklist below as a starting point for discussions between the operator, the depot designer, the civil contractor and the lathe manufacturer.

  • Track alignment – a straight, level section with enough length on both sides to position every vehicle type, including the longest train, over the machine.
  • Pit dimensions and foundation – a reinforced concrete pit to the machine supplier's drawings, with foundation loads, anchor positions and tolerances agreed in writing.
  • Rails over the machine – how the rail sections above the lathe are supported and moved, and how they interface with the depot track.
  • Vehicle movement – how trains will be drawn over the pit and indexed axle by axle: depot locomotive, rope or capstan system, or a Rail cum Road Vehicle (Shunter).
  • Services – electrical supply, earthing, compressed air where required, lighting, ventilation and data connections for remote diagnostics.
  • Drainage and chips – water and oil drainage from the pit and a practical route for removing chips.
  • Access and safety – stairs, walkways, guarding and lifting arrangements for maintenance, and clear separation between operators and moving vehicles.
  • Future capacity – space and services for a second machine or a tandem upgrade if fleet growth is expected.

Guide 3

Extending wheel life: re-profiling strategy

A wheel can only be re-profiled until it reaches its condemning diameter. Every millimetre of diameter removed unnecessarily shortens its life and brings forward the cost of new wheels or tyres and the downtime to fit them. The aim of a good re-profiling strategy is simple: restore a safe profile each time, with the least possible loss of diameter.

Flange thickness is usually what drives the cut. To rebuild a thin flange, the lathe must remove material from the tread until the full flange shape reappears – so a small amount of flange wear can cost several times as much diameter. That is why intervening at the right moment, and choosing the right target profile, matters so much.

  • Measure regularly and record every wheel, so trends become visible before limits are reached.
  • Set intervention limits below the safety limits, leaving room to plan work without emergency withdrawals.
  • Where the operator's standards allow, use approved intermediate profiles with reduced flange thickness to save diameter on a cut.
  • Machine flats and surface defects promptly; small defects grow and need deeper cuts later.
  • Keep diameter differences on an axle, a bogie and a vehicle within the specified tolerances.
  • Investigate root causes – brake adjustment, bogie alignment, wheel slide protection or track geometry – when one vehicle or route wears faster than the rest.

Building a wheel-life programme

A structured cycle turns re-profiling from an emergency task into a planned maintenance activity.

  1. 01

    Measure

    Capture diameter, flange height, flange thickness and flange slope for every wheel at fixed intervals.

  2. 02

    Trend

    Track wear rates by vehicle type, route and season to predict when each wheelset will need attention.

  3. 03

    Plan

    Schedule re-profiling before limits are reached and align it with other depot work.

  4. 04

    Machine minimally

    Let the lathe measure first and remove only the depth needed to restore the chosen profile.

  5. 05

    Record and review

    Store the post-machining measurements and review wheel life regularly to refine limits and profiles.
